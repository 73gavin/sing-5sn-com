Voice Range Test

Find the notes your voice can reach, and the ones it enjoys.

This test takes about 3 minutes. The app plays a note, you sing it back, and you say how it felt. Step by step it finds your lowest and highest notes and, more usefully, your comfortable range: the notes a song should stay inside.

  • Use headphones if you can, so the mic hears you and not the reference tone.
  • A quiet room helps. Sing on an open "ahh" at a normal speaking volume.
  • Don't push. If a note hurts, scratches, or cracks, mark it and stop.
  • Your audio never leaves this device. Nothing is recorded or uploaded.

Practice: match the note

Hold a key (click, touch, or type the letter shown) to hear a note. It plays for as long as you hold it. Then sing it back on an "ahh" and hold it steady. The needle shows how far flat or sharp you are in cents: 100 cents is one semitone (one piano key, about 5.9% in frequency), and under 10 cents (0.6%) is dead on.

Testing a phone, laptop, or mic: open this page on two devices, hold notes on one, and watch the other. A steady tone should read the right Hz with clarity near 1.00. Small speakers can't play the lowest notes, but their harmonics still carry the pitch.

Practice a song

Pick a song and press Sing it. After a few clicks to count you in, the notes scroll toward the blue line. Each bar is a note: higher bars are higher notes, longer bars are held longer. Sing each note as it crosses the line, and your voice draws over it.

      Timing looks off? Measure the delay

      Speakers, headphones, and mics all add a small delay, which can make every note look late. Measure it plays a short melody out loud and listens to it through the mic, the way it listens to you, then sets the adjustment so it scores on time. Take headphones off, turn the volume up, and stay quiet while it runs (about 12 seconds). Measuring again should then read close to 0 ms; readings within about 25 ms of each other are as close as it can tell.

      The measurement covers this speaker. Wired headphones use the same timing; Bluetooth headphones add their own delay (often 150-250 ms more), which the mic can't hear, so nudge it with Later. If notes still show late when you're sure you're on the beat, press Later; if early, press Earlier.

        New to music? How note names work

        Notes are named with letters C D E F G A B, plus "sharps" (C# = a half step above C) for the black piano keys. After B the letters start over, one octave higher. An octave is a doubling of frequency: A3 is 220 Hz, A4 is 440 Hz.

        The number is the octave. C4 is "middle C" (261.6 Hz), the middle of a piano. Most adult men speak around 85-155 Hz (roughly E2-D#3), most women around 165-255 Hz (E3-B3).

        A semitone (half step) is one piano key, black or white: a frequency ratio of about 1.059. 12 semitones make an octave. A typical pop melody spans 12-18 semitones.

        Comfortable range (singers call it tessitura) matters more than extremes. A song in the wrong key for you is the most common reason a song feels "unsingable". Any song can be moved (transposed) to fit your range.
